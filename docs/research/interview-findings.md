# Interview Semantic Analysis Summary

## Scope and headline finding

This working analysis covers four student interviews, labelled S1-S4. It uses a fixed codebook and a reproducible rule-assisted coding pass, followed by cross-case interpretation. Counts show how many participants expressed a code at least once, not how often a phrase appeared. This prevents a longer or more talkative interview from dominating the result.

The central finding is that students do not want a demand ranking that tells them what to learn. They want a decision aid that combines transparent labour-market evidence with personal relevance, time constraints, and concrete interpretation from a person with recent experience. The market metaphor attracts attention, but unexplained percentages, rankings, and deficit-style profiles can increase suspicion and career anxiety.

## Analysis method

1. The participant's speech was separated from interviewer speech.
2. Adjacent participant paragraphs were combined into a meaning unit until the interviewer resumed.
3. A frozen codebook defined each code, inclusion rule, exclusion rule, source anchors, and its link to U1-U9.
4. Exact rule matches produced candidate highlights and an auditable coded-segment table.
5. Code prevalence was calculated at participant level. A code is reported as 4/4 when all four participants expressed it at least once, regardless of repetition.
6. Related codes were grouped into six categories and six higher-order findings.
7. Contradictory or weaker cases were retained instead of being forced into the majority finding.
8. A second coder still needs to complete the prepared 25% stratified sample before the team can claim inter-coder agreement.

This is best described in the report as hybrid deductive-inductive qualitative content analysis or codebook thematic analysis. It should not be described as fully automated semantic analysis, and the current coding should not be described as independently validated until the second-coder step is complete.

## Grouped findings

### 1 Evidence must be both trustworthy and personally relevant

Evidence: informal or experiential sources appeared in 4/4 interviews; contextual filters appeared in 4/4; metric-definition or false-precision concerns appeared in 4/4; provenance and recency were explicitly requested by 3/4; careers-service mismatch appeared in 3/4.

Participants used peers, siblings, tutors, Reddit, LinkedIn, WeChat, job advertisements, and government information for different purposes. They did not treat one source as universally authoritative. S4 distinguished market-level and experience-level evidence directly: government data could show whether engineering jobs were growing, while a student or practitioner could say whether interns actually used a particular tool. S3 made the same distinction between the overall market and what to do next week. S1 said trust depended on where the displayed data came from. S2 described Australian CareerHub listings as poorly matched to international students and overseas contacts as difficult to apply to the Australian context.

Interpretation: trust is conditional on the question. Students need visible source, date range, location, role level, sample or advertisement count, and eligibility context. A precise percentage without those fields does not create reliable evidence.

Design consequence: every trend must expose its definition, denominator or base count, time window, geography, role level, and source. Default filters should include field, graduate or entry-level status, location, and work-rights eligibility where legally and ethically appropriate.

### 2 Demand is an input to a decision not the decision itself

Evidence: personal relevance or interest appeared in 4/4 interviews; time and prioritisation constraints in 3/4; career-direction uncertainty in 3/4; transferability as risk reduction in 3/4; explicit refusal to act on demand alone in 3/4.

S1 chose units that extended existing interests and said a 38% increase would not override what they wanted to learn. S3 considered whether a technology-law elective was future-proof but still relied on a recent student's experience. S4 chose Python partly because it preserved options if the preferred engineering direction changed. S4 also described having only four or five hours per week and being unable to learn five skills at once. S2's earlier degree choice was driven more by personal appeal and a sibling's experience than by labour-market evidence.

Interpretation: the actionable question is not "Which skill is rising fastest?" It is "Which option is relevant to my target path, feasible in the time I have, and still useful if my direction changes?"

Design consequence: recommendations should show relevance, expected learning effort, entry-level applicability, transferability, and why the item was prioritised. The interface should support comparison between two real options and should never equate high demand with a universal recommendation.

### 3 Human advice is valuable when access is safe and the answer is concrete

Evidence: the value of concrete practitioner information appeared in 4/4 interviews; cold-outreach friction in 3/4; opt-in or reason-based matching in 3/4; withdrawal after non-response in 3/4.

Participants wanted specific information such as day-to-day tasks, technologies actually used, internship expectations, system architecture, or one concrete example that could change the next action. Generic advice such as "work hard" or "network" was described as low value. S1 felt insufficiently skilled to approach a professional. S3 and S4 worried that a cold message would sound transactional or like a request for a job. S2, S3, and S4 indicated that an ignored request could reduce or stop future outreach.

Interpretation: the problem is not only finding professionals. The product must reduce uncertainty about whom to ask, why the match exists, what to ask, and whether contact is welcome.

Design consequence: professionals should opt in, publish availability and topics, and receive the student's decision context. Students should see the match reason and suggested questions. Declines and timeouts should be handled privately, with another option offered without framing the event as personal rejection.

### 4 The market metaphor is engaging but creates a higher explanation burden

Evidence: explicit recognition or preference for the market metaphor appeared in 3/4 interviews. Metric ambiguity appeared in 4/4. Refusal to act on demand alone appeared in 3/4.

S2, S3, and S4 recognised or preferred the stock-style presentation. S3 and S4 had prior investment experience, while S2 did not, showing that recognition is not restricted to investors in this small sample. However, all four interviews exposed a metric-definition problem: S1 questioned the difference between salary and entry-level views; S2 found the 38% comparison unclear; S3 asked what 38% measured; and S4 distinguished "up 38%" from "38% of jobs" and asked about the base count.

Interpretation: visual familiarity does not equal correct comprehension. Investment experience may improve recognition, but it does not remove ambiguity or guarantee appropriate action.

Design consequence: retain lightweight ideas such as watchlists and trends, but define every metric in plain language near the number. Avoid unnecessary decimal precision. Test comprehension using one-answer interpretation questions rather than asking whether the screen "makes sense".

### 5 Progress framing is safer than ranking or deficit framing

Evidence: progress or roadmap framing appeared in 4/4 interviews; anxiety or harmful comparison appeared in 3/4.

S1 described the first-year profile as frightening but potentially useful as a roadmap. S2 normalised beginner status and questioned whether students could meaningfully compare themselves without industry exposure. S3 rejected a career-readiness score and an ideal-student comparison. S4 said an empty profile felt like "you have no skills" and would delete an app that ranked them in the bottom 30%.

Interpretation: the same information can support action or amplify anxiety depending on framing. The strongest common direction is to present a starting point and next steps, not a deficit against peers.

Design consequence: do not show student percentiles, league tables, portfolio-performance scores, or a single career-readiness percentage. Show confirmed skills, suggested skills to explore, progress stages, and a small number of prioritised next actions. Normalise uncertainty and early-stage profiles.

### 6 Personalisation is acceptable only with data minimisation and correction controls

Evidence: acceptance of minimal career data appeared in 4/4 interviews; sensitive-data boundaries appeared in 4/4; editable uncertain inference appeared in 4/4.

Participants were generally willing to provide degree, year, units, skills, software, and career interests. Marks, detailed address, family information, visa status, and rejection history were treated as sensitive, conditional, or unnecessary. All four interviews supported an inference model only when guesses were presented as suggestions and could be confirmed, removed, or corrected.

Interpretation: personalisation is not blanket consent. Acceptance depends on purpose, visibility, necessity, and user control.

Design consequence: use progressive profiling. Label inferred skills as suggestions, request confirmation, and make removal immediate. Keep sensitive fields optional, explain why they improve results, and let users control whether a professional can see each field.

## Contradictions and unresolved questions

- The combined data-plus-person workflow is explicitly supported by S3 and S4, but not established by all four participants. This should be reported as promising rather than validated.
- S1 found careers workshops useful, while S2-S4 described eligibility, scope, or specificity limitations. The requirement is therefore not to replace careers services universally, but to address decision-specific gaps.
- S2 did not report the profile comparison as distressing and instead normalised being a beginner. This suggests supportive wording can reduce harm, but it does not cancel the stronger anxiety reactions in S1, S3, and S4.
- No professional interviews are present. U6, professional willingness and capacity, remains unresolved. Student willingness to contact someone cannot establish supply-side feasibility.
- All four participants are later-year students. The first-year profile reactions are hypothetical, so claims about actual first-year users require direct recruitment.

## Questionnaire–interview cross-validation

The six questionnaire responses were analysed separately and then compared with the interview findings at construct level. Five of six respondents said their degree had not sufficiently prepared them for the workforce. All six named technical or field-specific skills, and five named communication or interpersonal skills.

This converges with interview evidence of a preparation and prioritisation gap. The useful combined interpretation is not that students learned no technical skills: five of six questionnaire respondents reported at least partial technical attainment. Rather, the interviews explain the remaining gap as shallow knowledge across many options, missing practical context, limited time, generic employer language, and uncertainty about what to prioritise.

The sources also converge or complement each other on mixed technical/interpersonal expectations, the value of practical workplace context, and the need for discipline- and role-specific recommendations. The questionnaire did not measure trust, metric comprehension, networking friction, the market metaphor, anxiety, privacy, the data-plus-person workflow, or careers-service mismatch. Those findings remain interview-supported rather than cross-validated.

Because respondents cannot be safely linked across the two datasets, this is construct-level methodological triangulation, not participant-by-participant validation. See `QUESTIONNAIRE_INTERVIEW_CROSS_VALIDATION.md` and `12_interview_questionnaire_cross_validation.csv`.

## Data quality limitations

- The sample is four students and is not statistically representative.
- S1 and S2 contain identifiable speaker names and should be de-identified before they are included in a submission appendix.
- S2 contains timestamp fragments and several leading or evaluative interviewer responses. This reduces comparability and increases interviewer-bias risk.
- S3 and S4 are cleaner, structured transcripts and therefore contain more analysable meaning units. Participant-level prevalence is used to reduce this length and structure bias.
- The interview plan commits to two independent coders. That step has not yet been completed, so inter-coder agreement cannot yet be claimed.
- The product concept was shown during the interviews. Findings before and after concept exposure should be distinguished in the final report to avoid treating prompted reactions as spontaneous needs.
