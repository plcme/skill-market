# Questionnaire–Interview Cross-Validation

## What cross-validation means here

The questionnaire and interview data were analysed separately and then merged at the level of shared constructs. Because the questionnaire respondents cannot be safely or reliably matched to the interview participants, this is construct-level methodological triangulation rather than case-by-case validation.

The integration uses a convergence coding matrix. Each construct is classified as:

- convergence: both sources support a substantively similar finding;
- complementarity: the sources address the same issue from different angles;
- dissonance: the sources appear to disagree;
- silence: one source did not measure the construct.

Silence is not negative evidence. A questionnaire that did not ask about trust, networking or privacy cannot confirm or refute the corresponding interview findings.

## Questionnaire results

All six respondents consented. Five of six answered that their degree had not sufficiently prepared them for the workforce.

All six named at least one technical or field-specific capability that employers seek. Five named communication or interpersonal capability. Two named teamwork, two named practical experience or projects, and one each named problem-solving, adaptability and initiative.

The most useful combined finding is not that degrees teach no technical skills. Five of six respondents reported at least partial technical attainment, yet five of six still felt insufficiently prepared. This suggests a gap involving depth, breadth, practical context, confidence or prioritisation. The interviews explain that gap: participants described shallow knowledge across many tools, generic employer language, limited time, and uncertainty about what to learn next.

## Findings supported by both sources

### 1. A preparation and prioritisation gap

Questionnaire evidence: 5/6 reported insufficient preparation.

Interview evidence: S1 wanted a roadmap; S3 could not turn broad skill labels into action; S4 described knowing several tools only superficially and not knowing what to prioritise.

Integrated interpretation: the problem is not simply lack of information. Students need a small number of relevant, feasible next steps.

### 2. Employability combines technical and human capabilities

Questionnaire evidence: technical or field-specific skills appeared in 6/6 responses and communication or interpersonal skills in 5/6.

Interview evidence: S3 named communication, writing, technology literacy and data skills; S4 named data analysis, programming, CAD, project management and communication. S1 and S2 supplied additional technical examples.

Integrated interpretation: the system should not treat employability as a single universal technical-skill ranking. Recommendations need field and role context and must be able to represent both technical and interpersonal capabilities.

### 3. Practical context is a meaningful gap

Questionnaire evidence: two respondents explicitly named prior experience, projects or hands-on practice, and both reported that the degree had not supplied it.

Interview evidence: S1 valued people with relevant experience; S3 wanted concrete examples of junior work; S4 described the experience paradox and found day-to-day practitioner examples more useful than generic presentation material.

Integrated interpretation: concrete workplace examples and recent practitioner knowledge can bridge a gap that a skill list alone cannot address.

### 4. Recommendations must be context-sensitive

Questionnaire evidence: respondents came from engineering, computing, economics, finance and medicine and named substantially different capabilities.

Interview evidence: participants repeatedly required evidence to be interpreted for their own discipline, target role, level, location and available time.

Integrated interpretation: one global ranking should not be presented as universally actionable.

## Interview findings not validated by the questionnaire

The questionnaire did not measure:

- source trust, provenance, recency or percentage comprehension;
- cold-outreach friction, opt-in matching or non-response;
- recognition of the market metaphor;
- rankings, career anxiety or preferred progress framing;
- privacy boundaries or editable inferences;
- the connected data-plus-person workflow;
- careers-service mismatch.

These remain interview-supported findings. The questionnaire's general consent item must not be used as evidence of product-level privacy acceptance.

## Reporting recommendation

Use the wording “the questionnaire and interview findings converged on a perceived preparation and prioritisation gap” rather than “the questionnaire proved the interviews.” For constructs not asked in the questionnaire, state “not assessed in the questionnaire” rather than “not supported.”

The complete construct-by-construct evidence and limits are recorded in `12_interview_questionnaire_cross_validation.csv`.
